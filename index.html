One rule: nothing the AI says is evidence. Every answer gets a tag:

KNOWN evidence from real people: who, when, how many
ASSUMED our belief, desk research, or anything the AI suggested
UNKNOWN no answer yet - a good answer

Part 1 · Think with AI: let the chat interview you

Two pastes: the master prompt, then one framework under it. One person types, the team answers together.

1The master prompt

It turns a free chat into a skeptical co-founder who asks one question at a time, tags every answer, and won't fill anything in for you.

2Pick one framework for your stage

Open the one that fits the question your team is stuck on, copy it, and paste it under "FRAMEWORK:".

Is the problem worth solving? · 4 U's + 5 Whys (Michael Skok; Toyota)
What is this? A short explanation

Two quick checks that a problem is worth building a company on. The 4 U's ask whether it is Unworkable (it really breaks something or costs money), Unavoidable (people can't just skip it), Urgent (it is in their top 3 right now) and Underserved (what exists today isn't enough). The more U's, the stronger the problem.

The 5 Whys (from Toyota) keep asking "why does this happen?" until you reach the root cause. Example: cafeterias throw food away. Why? They cook for the lunch rush. Why? They can't tell how many people will come. That last answer may be the real problem.

Who exactly? · Beachhead market + persona (Bill Aulet, Disciplined Entrepreneurship, MIT)
What is this? A short explanation

Don't start with "everyone". A beachhead is the one small group you win first: they can pay, you can reach them, they need it badly, and winning them helps you win the next group.

A persona is one real person from that group, by name: their day, their habits, where they hang out. Example: not "students", but "first-year students in the dorms who eat on campus four times a week".

What do they really need? · Value Proposition Canvas (Strategyzer.com)
What is this? A short explanation

Two sides. On one side, the customer: the jobs they are trying to get done, their pains and the gains they want. On the other side, your offer: which pain each part of it removes, and which gain it creates.

You have a fit when your offer hits their top pains - not the ones you imagined. Start with the customer side; most teams start with their product.

Which solution? · Opportunity Solution Tree (Teresa Torres)
What is this? A short explanation

A tree that grows down from your goal. At the top: one measurable outcome. Under it: opportunities - real needs and pains you heard from customers. Under each opportunity: at least 3 different solutions, and under each solution, what must be true for it to work.

It stops you from falling in love with your first solution.

How does it make money? · Lean Canvas (Ash Maurya)
What is this? A short explanation

One page, nine boxes, that describes the whole business: problem, customers, value, solution, channels, revenue, costs, key numbers and unfair advantage. Fill it in the order given - problem and customers first.

Most boxes will be ASSUMED at first. That's the point: the canvas shows you where the holes are.

What could kill us? · Pre-mortem (Gary Klein)
What is this? A short explanation

Imagine it is one year from now and the startup has failed. Now explain why. Treating the failure as a fact makes people name risks they would otherwise keep quiet about.

Write your reasons alone first, then share them, and only then let the AI add what you missed.

Save your table. When the chat gives you the final table, copy it into a doc or a note. You'll need it in parts 3 and 4. If a free chat runs out of messages, paste the table into a new chat and continue.

3Three habits that make the chat more honest

  • Ask, don't tell: "Is it true that...?" instead of "Students need...".
  • Third person: "A student team proposes..." instead of "my idea".
  • Push back: reply "Are you sure?" If its critique collapses, it was weak.

Part 2 · Research outward

A language model is for ideas and questions. For facts, numbers and competitors, use a tool that shows its sources - and open them.

1Deep Research: a cited market report

Not during the workshop. One report can use up your free Gemini for the rest of the evening - and you need it for the practice. Watch the demo, and run it at home.

An agent reads dozens of sources for 5-10 minutes and writes a report with citations. At home, on a free account, try Gemini: choose Deep Research under Tools. Google says it may be unavailable at busy times. ChatGPT gives a limited number of free runs; Perplexity gives one a month.

What is this? A short explanation

An AI agent that runs dozens of searches by itself, reads the pages and writes a long report with a source for every claim - in 5-10 minutes. Good for a first map of a market.

It still makes mistakes, so open the sources before you trust a number.

Before you trust it: open 3 of its sources. Any number without a source is ASSUMED. Then ask it: "What did you NOT find?"

2Gemini Notebook: think across your own materials

Gemini Notebook (formerly NotebookLM) answers from your sources only, with citations. Make one notebook for your market and load: interview notes, competitor websites, papers, a YouTube video, your Deep Research report.

What is this? A short explanation

You upload your own materials - interview notes, websites, papers, videos - and it answers only from them, with a citation to the exact place. Good for finding patterns across many interviews, and for comparing competitors in their own words.

It is not a search engine: it knows only what you give it.

Also try: a mind map of your market, or an audio overview to listen to on the bus - it can be made in Arabic too.

3Quick checks with Perplexity

Competitors, prices, market size - fast, with links you can open.

What is this? A short explanation

A search engine that answers in sentences and shows the links it used. Good for quick facts: who the competitors are, what they charge, how big a market is.

Open the links - the summary can be wrong even when the link is right.

4The competitor no search will show you

This one is a thinking question, not a search - paste it into any chat.

What is this? A short explanation

Many AI startups are one feature that a big company could add in a week. If that is your idea, you have no business.

Ask it early, so you know what would still be hard to copy: your data, your relationships with customers, your access to a place or a community.

5Patents: has someone already done it?

A first sanity check, 15 minutes. Describe the idea without the secret part, and turn off training in your chat first (see "Your next week" below).

Each patent tool searches in its own way. Instead of guessing the right words, let a chat interview you about the idea, learn how each tool searches best, and write the query for you - one for PQAI, one for Google Patents, one for Perplexity Patents.

What is this? A short explanation

A patent is a public document: anyone can read any patent, for free. A search before you build shows whether someone already protected the idea - and how others solved the same problem.

Patents are written in their own dry language, so the same idea can hide behind other words. That's why it helps to let a chat write the queries.

Two words you'll meet: claim 1 - the first claim, the sentence that says what the patent actually protects; CPC - the subject code patent offices give every patent (for example, A23L is food). Searching by code finds patents that use other words.

Step 2 - search. Paste each query into its tool. On Google Patents, tick "Include non-patent literature". Results look off? Paste what you got back into the chat and ask it to sharpen the query.

Step 3 - read the 3 closest. For each: the abstract (the summary), claim 1 (what it protects), one drawing, the number, the date and the legal status (is it still in force?). Then click "Similar" on the closest one.

Know the limits. This is not legal advice, and finding nothing does not mean the idea is new. Chats invent patent numbers - open every number on Google Patents.

Part 3 · Test before you build

An MVP takes weeks. Your riskiest assumption can be tested this week - without code.

1What to test - and a number

Take the riskiest assumption from the end of your Part 1 table. Before you test it, write down which result means you're wrong - a real number. Otherwise every result looks like success.

Example: "Students would buy evening leftovers at half price." We put a poster with a sign-up link at the cafeteria for 3 days. If fewer than 20 students sign up, we were wrong.

2Option A · A landing page ("fake door")

A page for a product that doesn't exist yet, with a "join the waitlist" button. First make a short Google Form for the sign-ups, then build the page.

What is this? A short explanation

You build a page for a product that doesn't exist yet, with one button: "Join the waitlist". If people click and leave their email, they want it enough to act - that's stronger than anyone saying "nice idea".

It stays honest as long as the page says "coming soon" and you never take money.

How to share it: in Gemini, build it as an interactive app and export it to Google Sites (it starts as a private draft - publish it). In Claude, publish the artifact and copy the link. Or download the file and send it.
Be honest: the page says "coming soon". Never take money for something that doesn't exist.

3Option B · An interview script, and a rehearsal

What is this? A short explanation

A way to interview customers so that even your mother couldn't lie to you. Talk about their life, not your idea. Ask about the last time something happened, not about the future.

Compliments and "I would" are not evidence - what people did last week is.

What is this? A short explanation

A chat plays a customer so you can practise asking good questions before you meet real people. Type DEBRIEF and it grades your questions.

It is practice only: nothing it says is evidence.

Rehearsal, not evidence. AI customers are too positive and too shallow. Nothing they say is KNOWN. Use it to practise asking - then talk to real people.

Part 4 · Go outward

A pitch deck built from your table, so it says only what you know. Done early? Try a partner search, or a post that tests your idea.

1A pitch deck, built in a chat

Any free chat can build a deck as one HTML file: a web page that works like a slideshow. You don't need to read any code.

What is this? A short explanation

A short deck that tells an investor or a partner your story: the problem, your solution, why now, the market, the competition, how you make money, the team and what you're asking for.

Built from your table, it says only what you know.

Where to see it: Claude shows it as an artifact next to the chat; Gemini opens it in Canvas; in ChatGPT, open the preview of the code. All three let you download it. Open the file in a browser and press F11 for full screen.

2Extra · Finding partners

What is this? A short explanation

Start from who you already have: the people each teammate already knows. Then name the skill your team is missing, and look for people who have it.

A good first message is short, about them, and has one clear ask.

Free LinkedIn limits searches and personal notes each month - so make the first message count.

3Extra · A post that tests your idea

What is this? A short explanation

A post is a free test: tell the problem through one real story, ask readers about their own experience, and link to your waitlist.

The replies and the sign-ups tell you whether the problem is real for other people too.

2Keep your idea private

Free chats may use your conversations to train their models. Turn it off before you paste anything sensitive:

  • ChatGPT: Settings → Data controls → "Improve the model for everyone" off. Or use a temporary chat.
  • Gemini: Activity → turn off "Keep Activity". Or use a temporary chat.
  • Claude: Settings → Privacy → turn off model training. Or use an incognito chat.
  • Perplexity: Settings → turn off "AI data retention".
